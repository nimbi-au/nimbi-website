AML/CTF for accountants

An accounting practice is a reporting entity when it provides a designated service in the course of business, not because it is an accounting practice. Tax returns, financial statements, BAS and bookkeeping are not designated services on their own.

When the rules may apply

The professional services in table 6 of the Act that accountants most often provide are:

  • assisting a client to plan or carry out a transaction to buy, sell or transfer real estate, or a company, trust or other legal arrangement, other than under a court or tribunal order
  • receiving, holding, controlling or managing a client's money or other property as part of a transaction, including through a trust account. Holding money only as payment for your own services, or as an incidental part of a non-designated service, is excluded
  • assisting with equity or debt financing for a company or legal arrangement
  • assisting to create or restructure a company, trust or other legal arrangement, including SMSF trusts and corporate trustees. Testamentary trusts are excluded
  • acting as, or arranging for someone to act as, a director, secretary, trustee, partner or nominee shareholder for a client
  • providing a registered office or principal place of business address, or selling a shelf company.

AUSTRAC's test is whether your work directly advances the transaction or the creation of the structure. General advice that only influences what a client does is not enough. The service starts when you act on instructions and a transaction or structure is in train.

Examples

An accountant drafts and arranges execution of a trust deed to set up an SMSF: a designated service, from the point the instruction is accepted.

An accountant gives tax advice on the implications of selling a company, with no buyer in view: not a designated service. Once the accountant is instructed to act in the sale to an identified buyer, it is.

Client money sits in your trust account only to pay your own fees: not a designated service. Letting a client use the trust account like a bank account would be.

Your obligations in practice

The obligations are the same ten themes for every practice; only a few notes differ by sector. They are set out once, with the notes for accounting practices.

Know

Assess the risk your services, clients, channels and countries carry.

Set

Write the program: who does what, by when, approved by a senior manager.

Run

Check who you deal with, keep watching, report when the law requires, keep records.

Prove

Show AUSTRAC and an independent evaluator that the rules were followed.

Ten themes, grouped for readability. They are an editorial grouping of the Act and the AML/CTF Rules 2025, not a count of every statutory duty, and how each applies depends on the services you provide.

  1. Enrol with AUSTRAC. Apply to enrol no later than 28 days after you start providing a designated service, and update your details within 14 days of a change. Notify AUSTRAC of your compliance officer within the required time.
  2. Assess your risk. Identify and assess the money laundering, terrorism financing and proliferation financing risks of your services, customers, delivery channels and the countries involved, and write down why. Review it when things change and periodically.
  3. Keep a written program. AML/CTF policies, procedures, systems and controls that fit the nature, size and complexity of your practice, in place before you provide a designated service and followed in daily work.
  4. Approve and oversee it. A senior manager approves the program and its updates; a governing body oversees it. In a small practice one person may hold more than one role.
  5. Appoint a compliance officer. A fit and proper person at management level who oversees day-to-day compliance and reports to the governing body.
  6. Know your customer before you start. Before the designated service begins, establish on reasonable grounds who the customer is, who owns or controls a company or trust, who acts for them, whether anyone is a politically exposed person or subject to sanctions, and the nature and purpose of the relationship. Collect and verify what the customer's risk calls for. Simplified measures are available only where the risk is low, enhanced measures are not required and your policies provide for them; enhanced measures, including source of funds and wealth where relevant, are required where the Act triggers them, including high risk, a foreign politically exposed person or a suspicion you continue to act through.
  7. Keep watching. Ongoing due diligence for as long as the relationship lasts: monitor for unusual transactions and behaviour, update information, re-check screening and respond to changes in risk. Clients you already had before 1 July 2026 are covered by the Act's provisions for pre-commencement customers rather than full initial due diligence from day one; check those provisions before re-verifying your whole client book.
  8. Report when required. A suspicious matter report where you suspect on reasonable grounds that a customer is not who they claim to be or that the service relates to money laundering, terrorism financing, proceeds of crime or another offence: within three business days after the day you form the suspicion, or 24 hours if it concerns terrorism financing. An annual compliance report. A threshold transaction report applies only if your practice receives or pays physical currency of $10,000 or more in a designated service; ordinary electronic payments are not threshold transactions. An unusual event or a screening match is not automatically a report; a person assesses whether there are reasonable grounds.
  9. Keep records, check and train people. Due diligence records for seven years after the relationship ends or the last occasional transaction, and program and other required records for the periods the Act sets. Risk-based due diligence on personnel in AML/CTF roles, and training appropriate to those roles, at a frequency your program sets.
  10. Independent evaluation. At least once every three years, at a frequency your policies set; transitional rules stagger the first one for newly regulated practices. Act on the findings.
What a transaction looks like in an accounting practice

For an accounting practice, the events that matter are the designated services themselves and the movements through the trust account, rather than a stream of payments. Your risk assessment sets out which of these carry more risk for your practice and what you will look for. Typical examples: layered or offshore structures with no commercial reason; reluctance to name a beneficial owner; deposits from unrelated third parties; an overpayment followed by a request to refund to a different account; a sale price out of line with the market; loans with no repayment terms; fees paid in cash or by a third party.
