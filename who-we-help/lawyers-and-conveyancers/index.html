AML/CTF for lawyers and conveyancers

The Act regulates a subset of what a legal or conveyancing practice does, not the profession. Advice, litigation and dispute resolution on their own are generally outside it; acting in a transaction or creating a structure is inside it.

When the rules may apply

The professional services in table 6 of the Act that legal and conveyancing practices most often provide are:

  • acting for a client in a transaction to buy, sell or transfer real estate: the ordinary conveyancing steps, from preparing the contract once a price is agreed to settlement. Transfers under a court or tribunal order are excluded, as are leases of 30 years or less
  • acting for a client in the sale, purchase or transfer of a controlling interest in a company, trust or other legal arrangement
  • receiving, holding, controlling or managing client money or property as part of a transaction, including through your trust account. Money held only as payment for your services, incidental holdings for non-designated work, and receipts or payments under a court order are excluded
  • assisting with equity or debt financing for a company or legal arrangement
  • assisting to create or restructure a company, trust or other legal arrangement. Wills and testamentary trusts are excluded
  • acting as, or arranging, a director, secretary, trustee, partner, attorney of an entity or nominee shareholder, or providing a registered office or principal place of business address.

A conveyancer starts providing the service when acting on instructions and a transaction exists, typically when a price is agreed or the hammer falls at auction. Initial due diligence can be delayed in limited circumstances, such as after an auction, where completing it first would disrupt the ordinary course of business and the extra risk is low.

Examples

A conveyancer prepares and lodges the transfer for a family home given to a child for no payment: a designated service. Consideration is not required.

A family lawyer drafts consent orders that include a property transfer, and the transfer is then made under the court's order: not a designated service for either the lawyer or the conveyancer acting under the order.

A criminal defence practice holds money in trust to pay a barrister and provides no other designated service: the trust holding is incidental and excluded.

A town agent attends settlement for another conveyancer: the engaging conveyancer is the reporting entity and must cover the agent in its personnel due diligence and training.

Your obligations in practice

The obligations are the same ten themes for every practice; only a few notes differ by sector. They are set out once, with the notes for legal and conveyancing practices.

Know

Assess the risk your services, clients, channels and countries carry.

Set

Write the program: who does what, by when, approved by a senior manager.

Run

Check who you deal with, keep watching, report when the law requires, keep records.

Prove

Show AUSTRAC and an independent evaluator that the rules were followed.

Ten themes, grouped for readability. They are an editorial grouping of the Act and the AML/CTF Rules 2025, not a count of every statutory duty, and how each applies depends on the services you provide.

  1. Enrol with AUSTRAC. Apply to enrol no later than 28 days after you start providing a designated service, and update your details within 14 days of a change. Notify AUSTRAC of your compliance officer within the required time.
  2. Assess your risk. Identify and assess the money laundering, terrorism financing and proliferation financing risks of your services, customers, delivery channels and the countries involved, and write down why. Review it when things change and periodically.
  3. Keep a written program. AML/CTF policies, procedures, systems and controls that fit the nature, size and complexity of your practice, in place before you provide a designated service and followed in daily work.
  4. Approve and oversee it. A senior manager approves the program and its updates; a governing body oversees it. In a small practice one person may hold more than one role.
  5. Appoint a compliance officer. A fit and proper person at management level who oversees day-to-day compliance and reports to the governing body.
  6. Know your customer before you start. Before the designated service begins, establish on reasonable grounds who the customer is, who owns or controls a company or trust, who acts for them, whether anyone is a politically exposed person or subject to sanctions, and the nature and purpose of the relationship. Collect and verify what the customer's risk calls for. Simplified measures are available only where the risk is low, enhanced measures are not required and your policies provide for them; enhanced measures, including source of funds and wealth where relevant, are required where the Act triggers them, including high risk, a foreign politically exposed person or a suspicion you continue to act through.
  7. Keep watching. Ongoing due diligence for as long as the relationship lasts: monitor for unusual transactions and behaviour, update information, re-check screening and respond to changes in risk. Clients you already had before 1 July 2026 are covered by the Act's provisions for pre-commencement customers rather than full initial due diligence from day one; check those provisions before re-verifying your whole client book.
  8. Report when required. A suspicious matter report where you suspect on reasonable grounds that a customer is not who they claim to be or that the service relates to money laundering, terrorism financing, proceeds of crime or another offence: within three business days after the day you form the suspicion, or 24 hours if it concerns terrorism financing. An annual compliance report. A threshold transaction report applies only if your practice receives or pays physical currency of $10,000 or more in a designated service; ordinary electronic payments are not threshold transactions. Where information is subject to legal professional privilege, the reporting rules and timing differ: see privilege and reporting in the lawyers and conveyancers guide. An unusual event or a screening match is not automatically a report; a person assesses whether there are reasonable grounds.
  9. Keep records, check and train people. Due diligence records for seven years after the relationship ends or the last occasional transaction, and program and other required records for the periods the Act sets. Risk-based due diligence on personnel in AML/CTF roles, and training appropriate to those roles, at a frequency your program sets.
  10. Independent evaluation. At least once every three years, at a frequency your policies set; transitional rules stagger the first one for newly regulated practices. Act on the findings.
Privilege and reporting

The Act does not take away the right to withhold information that is subject to legal professional privilege, and privilege affects reporting in defined ways. If all of the information behind a suspicion is privileged, no suspicious matter report and no privilege form are required. If only part of it is privileged, you lodge the report with the non-privileged information together with AUSTRAC's legal professional privilege form for what is withheld. A privilege claim in a report can be made only where the privilege belongs to someone else, such as your client. The ordinary time is three business days after the day you form the suspicion; where part of the information is privileged and a form accompanies the report, it is five business days; terrorism financing remains 24 hours. Privilege is narrower than the general duty of confidentiality and does not cover communications made for an improper purpose. It does not affect enrolment or customer due diligence, and tipping-off restrictions still apply. See AUSTRAC's legal professional privilege guidance before setting your procedures.
