AML/CTF for accountants

An accounting practice is a reporting entity when it provides a designated service in the course of business, not because it is an accounting practice. Tax returns, financial statements, BAS and bookkeeping are not designated services on their own.

When the rules may apply

The professional services in table 6 of the Act that accountants most often provide are:

  • assisting a client to plan or carry out a transaction to buy, sell or transfer real estate, or a company, trust or other legal arrangement, other than under a court or tribunal order
  • receiving, holding, controlling or managing a client's money or other property as part of a transaction, including through a trust account. Holding money only as payment for your own services, or as an incidental part of a non-designated service, is excluded
  • assisting with equity or debt financing for a company or legal arrangement
  • assisting to create or restructure a company, trust or other legal arrangement, including SMSF trusts and corporate trustees. Testamentary trusts are excluded
  • acting as, or arranging for someone to act as, a director, secretary, trustee, partner or nominee shareholder for a client
  • providing a registered office or principal place of business address, or selling a shelf company.

AUSTRAC's test is whether your work directly advances the transaction or the creation of the structure. General advice that only influences what a client does is not enough. The service starts when you act on instructions and a transaction or structure is in train.

Examples

An accountant drafts and arranges execution of a trust deed to set up an SMSF: a designated service, from the point the instruction is accepted.

An accountant gives tax advice on the implications of selling a company, with no buyer in view: not a designated service. Once the accountant is instructed to act in the sale to an identified buyer, it is.

Client money sits in your trust account only to pay your own fees: not a designated service. Letting a client use the trust account like a bank account would be.

What a transaction looks like in an accounting practice

For an accounting practice, the events that matter are the designated services themselves and the movements through the trust account, rather than a stream of payments. Your risk assessment sets out which of these carry more risk for your practice and what you will look for. Typical examples: layered or offshore structures with no commercial reason; reluctance to name a beneficial owner; deposits from unrelated third parties; an overpayment followed by a request to refund to a different account; a sale price out of line with the market; loans with no repayment terms; fees paid in cash or by a third party.
