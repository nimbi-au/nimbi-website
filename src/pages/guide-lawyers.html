Start with the work you do

The services you provide decide your obligations under the AML/CTF Act and Rules. Start with the work below, then check the exact definitions, the link to Australia and any exemptions.

Property and business matters

Acting on property transfers or the sale, purchase or transfer of a controlling interest in a legal entity or arrangement.

Structures, finance and appointments

You set up or restructure entities, help arrange finance, or provide officeholders or a registered address.

Client money and property

Trust account and other client-asset services that help a transaction proceed.

Advice, litigation and dispute resolution are generally outside scope on their own. Court orders, wills and some trust holdings need a matter-by-matter check.

Which services and exceptions should we check?
  • acting for a client in a transaction to buy, sell or transfer real estate: the ordinary conveyancing steps, from preparing the contract once a price is agreed to settlement. Services implementing a court or tribunal order already made are excluded, as are leases of 30 years or less
  • acting for a client in the sale, purchase or transfer of a controlling interest in a company, trust or other legal arrangement
  • receiving, holding and controlling, or managing client money or property as part of a transaction, including through your trust account. Money held only as payment for your services and relevant receipts or payments under a court order are excluded. Incidental holdings for non-designated work are exempt only where the entity provides no other designated services beyond this client-property service, assessed across the whole business
  • assisting with equity or debt financing for a company or legal arrangement
  • assisting to create or restructure a company, trust or other legal arrangement. Wills and testamentary trusts are excluded
  • acting as, or arranging, a director, secretary, trustee, partner, attorney of an entity or nominee shareholder, or providing a registered office or principal place of business address.

These services must be provided in the course of business and have a geographical link to Australia. A court-order exclusion does not automatically cover work done to obtain the order. Check the precise service and any applicable exemption.

When does the work come into scope?

A conveyancer starts providing the service when acting on instructions and a transaction exists, typically when a price is agreed or the hammer falls at auction. Initial due diligence can be delayed in limited circumstances, such as after an auction, where completing it first would disrupt the ordinary course of business and the extra risk is low.

A conveyancer prepares and lodges the transfer for a family home given to a child for no payment: a designated service. Consideration is not required.

A conveyancer implements a property transfer under a court order already made: that transfer service is excluded. Legal work undertaken before the order needs its own scope assessment.

A criminal defence practice holds money in trust to pay a barrister and provides no other designated service: the trust holding is incidental and excluded.

A town agent attends settlement for another conveyancer: the engaging conveyancer is the reporting entity and must cover the agent in its personnel due diligence and training.

How do privilege and reporting fit together?

Privilege arises only from confidential communications with a lawyer, so these rules apply in full to a legal practice and to a conveyancer who is a legal practitioner. A licensed conveyancer's own dealings with a client are not privileged; the rules below matter to a conveyancing practice only where it holds material that is privileged in the client's hands, such as a lawyer's advice the client has passed on. The Act does not take away the right to withhold information that is subject to legal professional privilege, and privilege affects reporting in defined ways. If all of the information behind a suspicion is privileged, no suspicious matter report and no privilege form are required. If only part of it is privileged, you lodge the report with the non-privileged information together with AUSTRAC's legal professional privilege form for what is withheld. A privilege claim in a report can be made only where the privilege belongs to someone else, such as your client. The ordinary time is three business days after the day you form the suspicion; where part of the information is privileged and a form accompanies the report, it is five business days; terrorism financing remains 24 hours. Privilege is narrower than the general duty of confidentiality and does not cover communications made for an improper purpose. It does not affect enrolment or customer due diligence, and tipping-off restrictions still apply. See AUSTRAC's legal professional privilege guidance before setting your procedures.

Recognise the risk in everyday work

Warning signs to look into, not findings of wrongdoing. Your team checks the facts and follows your procedures.

Warning sign

Instructions from an unclear third party

Someone directs the matter, but their authority or link to the client is unclear.

Your team’s response
Establish who the client is, verify the authority to act, and work out who owns or controls the entity.

Warning sign

A trust account used without a clear reason

A client asks you to receive or move money with no clear link to the matter.

Your team’s response
Ask the purpose and source of the money, then follow your risk and escalation steps.

Warning sign

Pressure to bypass the checks

A client pushes to proceed fast while holding back identity or ownership details.

Your team’s response
Close the gaps and rate the risk. Handle confidentiality, and privilege where it applies, through your written procedures.
