AML/CTF for lawyers and conveyancers

The Act regulates a subset of what a legal or conveyancing practice does, not the profession. Advice, litigation and dispute resolution on their own are generally outside it; acting in a transaction or creating a structure is inside it.

When the rules may apply

The professional services in table 6 of the Act that legal and conveyancing practices most often provide are:

  • acting for a client in a transaction to buy, sell or transfer real estate: the ordinary conveyancing steps, from preparing the contract once a price is agreed to settlement. Transfers under a court or tribunal order are excluded, as are leases of 30 years or less
  • acting for a client in the sale, purchase or transfer of a controlling interest in a company, trust or other legal arrangement
  • receiving, holding, controlling or managing client money or property as part of a transaction, including through your trust account. Money held only as payment for your services, incidental holdings for non-designated work, and receipts or payments under a court order are excluded
  • assisting with equity or debt financing for a company or legal arrangement
  • assisting to create or restructure a company, trust or other legal arrangement. Wills and testamentary trusts are excluded
  • acting as, or arranging, a director, secretary, trustee, partner, attorney of an entity or nominee shareholder, or providing a registered office or principal place of business address.

A conveyancer starts providing the service when acting on instructions and a transaction exists, typically when a price is agreed or the hammer falls at auction. Initial due diligence can be delayed in limited circumstances, such as after an auction, where completing it first would disrupt the ordinary course of business and the extra risk is low.

Examples

A conveyancer prepares and lodges the transfer for a family home given to a child for no payment: a designated service. Consideration is not required.

A family lawyer drafts consent orders that include a property transfer, and the transfer is then made under the court's order: not a designated service for either the lawyer or the conveyancer acting under the order.

A criminal defence practice holds money in trust to pay a barrister and provides no other designated service: the trust holding is incidental and excluded.

A town agent attends settlement for another conveyancer: the engaging conveyancer is the reporting entity and must cover the agent in its personnel due diligence and training.

Privilege and reporting

The Act does not take away the right to withhold information that is subject to legal professional privilege, and privilege affects reporting in defined ways. If all of the information behind a suspicion is privileged, no suspicious matter report and no privilege form are required. If only part of it is privileged, you lodge the report with the non-privileged information together with AUSTRAC's legal professional privilege form for what is withheld. A privilege claim in a report can be made only where the privilege belongs to someone else, such as your client. The ordinary time is three business days after the day you form the suspicion; where part of the information is privileged and a form accompanies the report, it is five business days; terrorism financing remains 24 hours. Privilege is narrower than the general duty of confidentiality and does not cover communications made for an improper purpose. It does not affect enrolment or customer due diligence, and tipping-off restrictions still apply. See AUSTRAC's legal professional privilege guidance before setting your procedures.
